# College-Prep Milestones — The 9th→12th Roadmap

**Workstream:** WS2 Future Planning · **Manager: Ivy League Guidance Counselor**
**Status: SIGNED OFF** · **Date:** 2026-10-11
**Parent context:** W5 ("Will my child get into a good college?") of the Pookie vision. This is system-prompt source of truth for all college-prep guidance the AI gives.

---

## The one truth I tell every family first

The transcript is the application. Everything else is commentary. A counselor reading a file spends the first sixty seconds on one thing: grades, in what courses, trending which direction. Essays get read, recommendations get skimmed, activities get scanned — but the transcript decides which pile the file lands in. Every milestone below exists to protect and build that transcript.

Second truth: colleges evaluate rigor *relative to what the school offers*, not relative to the internet. Five APs at a school that offers six is more impressive than seven APs at a school that offers twenty-two. The AI must always know the student's school profile before opining on rigor. "More APs" is not a strategy. "The hardest courses this student can earn A's and B's in, trending upward" is the strategy.

## 9th grade — Foundation

**What matters most:** habits and the GPA floor. Colleges see these grades. The story of "9th grade doesn't count" is false at every selective school and lazy everywhere else.

**The checklist:**
- Meet the school counselor in the first month. Learn the graduation requirements and the school's course-progression lanes (especially the math lane — the single most path-determining choice in high school).
- Build the study system now (see `study-skills.md`), while the content is forgiving. 9th grade is where the *system* gets installed; 11th grade is where it gets stress-tested.
- Try two to three activities; expect to keep one. Breadth is fine in 9th — it's the sampling year.
- Read the school's course catalog like a four-year map, not a one-year menu. Know which doors each choice opens.
- Summer after 9th: explore. A camp, a job, a deep dive into something curious. No resume-building yet — curiosity is the resume at this stage.

**Common mistakes families make:**
- Treating 9th grade as a warm-up and arriving in 10th needing "recovery" — recovery narratives are expensive.
- Letting the student pick courses for friends or for ease, then discovering junior year that the math lane dead-ends below calculus.
- Starting test prep. It's too early and it burns the student out before the tests that count.

## 10th grade — Trajectory

**What matters most:** proving trajectory. Sophomore year is when the AI and the family should be able to answer: *what direction is this transcript moving, and what is the plan for the two years colleges weigh most?*

**The checklist:**
- **Course-direction decisions for 11th grade happen this year.** The big ones: the math lane (for a STEM/pre-med student, the goal is calculus by 12th grade — the sequence decision is made *now*), the science sequence (biology → chemistry → physics is the expected spine; AP sciences slot in where the student has shown they can carry them), and the first real AP load.
- **The Rigor Rule (Senior-Manager-ratified, see coordination notes):** the student takes the hardest courses in which they can realistically earn A's and B's, evidenced by current performance — never courses chosen to impress. Rigor is earned by the transcript, not imposed on it.
- **PSAT in the fall of 10th grade** — as practice only. It does not count for National Merit. Its job is a diagnostic baseline: which test (SAT vs. ACT) and which subscore gaps to work on.
- **Extracurricular direction locks in.** By spring of 10th, the student should have one or two commitments they're going *deep* on (see the Spike section). Joining six clubs in 11th grade reads as exactly what it is.
- **Summer after 10th:** the first structured summer. Test-prep begins in earnest (light, steady — 30–45 minutes a few times a week, folded into the study system, not a boot camp), plus one spike-deepening activity.
- **Start the story bank** (see Essay Strategy): a running note of moments, observations, and small stories. Nobody remembers junior year clearly in the fall of senior year.

**Common mistakes families make:**
- Loading APs to "look rigorous" while the GPA craters. Admissions officers read the *grade*, not the ambition. A B in AP is not worth an A in honors if the student is drowning.
- Ignoring the sophomore PSAT, then meeting the SAT cold in junior year.
- Treating extracurriculars as a checklist to be completed in 11th grade. Depth takes years; it cannot be backfilled.

### Worked example: Dawson (10th grade, fall 2026, Class of 2028)

Dawson is STEM-leaning with an anesthesiologist goal. Current transcript: Chinese III 97.1, Lit&Comp 84.2, APCSP 80.2, EnvSci 79.0, Gov 79.0, Geometry 69.5.

**His #1 college-prep action this year is not a test, an essay, or an activity. It is Geometry recovery.** A sub-70 in the sophomore math course is the single loudest signal on his future transcript, and for a pre-med track — where every science course ahead assumes math fluency — it is also a skills problem, not just a number. The path-to-90 plan for Geometry (see `study-skills.md`) *is* his college-prep plan right now. Everything else is secondary.

**His 11th-grade course decisions (made spring 2027):**
- **Math lane:** Geometry → Algebra 2 (or the school's next step toward pre-calculus). The goal: pre-calculus or calculus in 12th. This decision is made with his counselor, and it depends on the Geometry recovery holding.
- **Science:** Chemistry in 11th — the expected spine, and the course that most predicts pre-med viability. Biology or AP Biology can follow; physics should appear by 12th.
- **AP CSP exam (May 2027):** he's in the course now; sitting the exam converts a class into a credential. Decide by the registration deadline based on his trajectory.
- **Rigor posture:** hold current rigor, add selectively in 11th *only where the transcript shows he can carry it*. No AP arms race while four core classes sit in the 70s–low 80s. Protect the floor first.

**His testing line:** PSAT fall 2026 (practice, diagnostic). PSAT/NMSQT fall 2027 (junior year — the one that counts for National Merit). SAT or ACT: diagnostic both by early 2028, first sitting spring 2028, one planned retake early fall 2028 if subscore gaps are closable.

**His spike:** healthcare/STEM. HOSA (Health Occupations Students of America) if Walton has a chapter; hospital volunteering at 16+; science fair or independent project; shadowing arranged through family network. During the grounding period (through Dec 18, 2026), the spike goes internal: health-science reading, Khan Academy chemistry preview, a small writing project. Depth doesn't require leaving the house.

## 11th grade — The Year That Counts Most

**What matters most:** junior-year grades are the last full year of grades most colleges see before deciding. This is the transcript's center of gravity. Everything else this year serves it.

**The checklist:**
- **Grades first, always.** The single highest-ROI activity of 11th grade is protecting the GPA. Test prep, activities, and essays all yield to it.
- **Standardized testing timeline:**
  - **Fall:** PSAT/NMSQT in October — the National Merit qualifying sitting. Prep for it lightly over the summer; it doubles as SAT prep.
  - **Spring:** first SAT or ACT sitting, March–May. Which test? Take a full timed diagnostic of each by January, pick the one with the better baseline *and* the better feel, then commit. Splitting prep across both tests is the classic waste.
  - **Retake logic:** one retake is normal and expected. A second retake is justified only if (a) there are specific, fixable subscore gaps, (b) the student will invest 40+ real hours between sittings, and (c) the target schools' 25th–75th bands show the current score below the school's middle 50%. Three sittings is the ceiling — beyond that, diminishing returns and a tired student. Never sit a test "just to see."
  - **Test-optional honesty:** test-optional is not test-blind. At most selective schools, a strong score still helps and a missing score is read in context. The AI's rule: submit if the score is at or above the school's 25th percentile for the student's band; withhold below it. Cite the band with its as-of date.
- **College list building (spring):** a research project, not a vibe. 8–12 schools across reach/match/likely bands (see Honesty Rules — bands, never probabilities). Include at least two the student would be *happy* to attend and can afford. For Georgia families: UGA, Georgia Tech, and Georgia State anchor the public list; **HOPE Scholarship (3.0 GPA) and Zell Miller (3.7 GPA + qualifying test score) change the math enormously** — the AI states both thresholds and the student's current standing against them, plainly.
- **Recommendation letters:** the ask goes to junior-year core-subject teachers, in spring. The student should have a real relationship *before* the ask — that means participating, visiting office hours, and being memorable for the right reasons starting in the fall. Provide each recommender a brag sheet (accomplishments, intended major, a specific classroom moment they might remember). Ask in person, follow up in writing, thank them in writing.
- **Essay seeds:** the narrative through-line starts forming now. Not the essay itself — the *material*. What has this student actually cared about, done, noticed? The story bank from 10th grade becomes the essay brainstorm of summer.
- **Demonstrated interest:** for schools that track it — open the emails, attend the virtual info session, visit if feasible, sign in at the college fair. The AI tracks which schools on the list consider demonstrated interest and nudges the specific actions.
- **Summer after 11th:** the production summer. A job, an internship, a research experience, a serious project — something with a *story*. This is the last summer that appears on the application. It should connect to the spike.

**Common mistakes families make:**
- Sacrificing junior grades for test prep. The transcript outweighs the score.
- Building the list from rankings instead of fit and finances. A list with no affordable likely-school is not a list, it's a wish.
- Starting essays in October of senior year. The personal statement needs months, not weeks.
- ED without understanding it's binding. (See 12th grade.)

## 12th grade — Execution

**What matters most:** flawless execution on deadlines, essays that sound like a human being, and no senioritis on the final transcript (colleges rescind).

**The timeline:**
- **Summer before 12th:** personal-statement draft done before school starts. College list finalized. Activity descriptions drafted (150 characters each — harder than it looks).
- **August–October:** Early Action / Early Decision deadlines (typically Nov 1 / Nov 15). **ED is binding** — the family must be able to afford the school without comparing aid offers, because there will be no comparison. The AI states this explicitly, every time, before any ED recommendation.
- **November–January:** Regular Decision deadlines. Supplements (each school's extra essays) are the hidden workload — budget one week per school's set.
- **October–March:** FAFSA and CSS Profile (for schools that require it). File early; aid is first-come at many schools. Georgia families: HOPE/Zell Miller eligibility confirmed through the state application.
- **Transcripts and score sends:** the school sends the transcript; the student sends the scores. The AI orchestrates — checklists, deadlines, confirmation tracking — because **there is no API that pushes these** (see Honesty Rules). Manual verification: "the portal shows received" is the only done that counts.
- **Spring:** decisions, financial-aid comparison (compare net cost, not sticker price; compare debt, not just aid), campus visits for admitted students, deposit by May 1. Waitlist strategy for the top choice only. Then: the final transcript goes out clean. Senioritis that drops grades risks rescission letters — real ones.

**Common mistakes families make:**
- Treating EA/ED as "early = better odds" without understanding the binding and financial implications.
- Writing the essay about what the student thinks colleges want to hear. Admissions officers read thousands; performed sincerity is detectable.
- Comparing sticker prices instead of net costs. A $80k school with $60k aid beats a $30k school with $5k aid.
- Letting the student mentally check out after acceptances. The final transcript still ships.

## Extracurricular strategy — the spike

**The concept:** admissions offices remember *spikes*, not well-roundedness. A spike is one or two areas pursued with unusual depth over multiple years — escalating commitment, real outcomes, evidence of genuine drive. Eight clubs with no depth is invisible. One deep pursuit with a story is memorable.

**What depth looks like, concretely:**
- Years 1–2: participate, learn, show up consistently.
- Year 3: lead, teach, organize, produce something (a project, an event, a result).
- Year 4: the thing has a shape a stranger could describe — "she built," "he led," "they founded."

**Leadership = evidence of initiative, not a title.** "President" of a club that met twice is worth less than the sophomore who organized the supply drive without a title. The AI looks for verbs, not nouns.

**For the STEM/pre-med student specifically:** HOSA or Science Olympiad, hospital volunteering (age 16+ at most hospitals — plan the birthday, not the wish), a science-fair or independent research project, shadowing a physician (arranged through network, with HIPAA-aware expectations — observation only), tutoring younger students in math/science (teaching is depth). One or two of these, sustained, beats five.

**The grounded-season rule:** when a student can't do activities (grounding, injury, family circumstances), the spike goes internal — reading, online coursework, writing, skill-building. Depth is a habit of attention, not a calendar of events. The AI should always have an internal-spike plan ready.

## Essay strategy

**The narrative through-line:** the application's essays should feel like they were written by the same person who lived the activities list. Not a brand, not a persona — coherence. The through-line is discovered, not manufactured: it emerges from the story bank (10th grade), the junior-year reflection, and the honest question "what do you actually care about?"

**The timeline:**
- **Spring 11th:** brainstorming. 20+ topic ideas, no judgment. The best essays usually come from idea #14, not idea #2.
- **Summer before 12th:** draft the personal statement. Write it fast, revise it slow. Read it aloud — if it doesn't sound like the student talking, it's wrong.
- **Fall 12th:** polish + supplements. Supplements answer "why us" — which requires actual research, not flattery. Name the professor, the lab, the program. Specificity is sincerity.

**What the AI does and doesn't do:** the AI brainstorms, gives feedback on drafts (structure, voice, specificity), and flags clichés. It never writes the essay. A manufactured essay is both detectable and a betrayal of the process — and the student has to live in the voice they submit.

## Demonstrated interest

Some colleges track it; many don't. The AI knows which schools on the student's list consider it (from Common Data Set section C7 — cited with as-of date) and assigns only the actions that count: open emails, attend info sessions (virtual counts), visit and sign in, meet the rep at the college fair, apply Early Action where offered. It never manufactures interest theater at schools that don't track it — that's wasted hours.

## §H — Honesty rules (non-negotiable, from the vision)

These govern every college-related statement the AI makes, to parent or child:

1. **Data sources are real and cited:** IPEDS, the College Scorecard API, and Common Data Set filings. Every statistic carries an as-of date. "UGA's middle-50% SAT is 1250–1430 (Common Data Set 2024–25, published fall 2025)" — never a bare number.
2. **Bands, never point probabilities.** The AI speaks in reach/match/likely bands derived from published ranges. It never says "you have a 63% chance." Nobody knows that number, including the college.
3. **No "guarantee" language, ever.** No safety is safe until the acceptance letter. Financial "guarantees" about aid are likewise banned.
4. **No fake integrations.** Common App, College Board, and ACT expose no third-party APIs. The AI orchestrates: checklists, deadline tracking with 30/14/7/2-day reminders, per-college requirements, essay workspace, recommender tracking, transcript-request workflows with the counselor, manual score entry with report verification. It never claims to submit, sync, or pull from these systems.
5. **Test-optional honesty:** test-optional ≠ test-blind. The AI explains what each school actually does with missing scores, from published policy.
6. **Affordability is part of every list.** Net-price calculators exist; the AI uses them. A list without affordable options is malpractice.

## What the AI does per grade (behavioral spec)

- **9th:** installs the study system; maps the four-year course lanes; starts the story bank. Proactive trigger: course-selection season — "here are the lanes and the doors each opens."
- **10th:** monitors trajectory (grade trend > snapshot); runs the PSAT diagnostic read; prompts the 11th-grade course decisions with the Rigor Rule; assigns the internal/external spike plan. Proactive trigger: spring course selection — the math-lane decision gets a dedicated parent briefing.
- **11th:** guards junior grades above all; runs the testing timeline (registration deadlines are the AI's job to never miss); builds the list as a research project with cited bands; manages recommender asks; starts essay brainstorming. Proactive trigger: any junior-grade slip >3 points fires the path-to-90 workflow before it fires a college-anxiety workflow — the transcript is the intervention.
- **12th:** deadline command center. Every date tracked, every portal verified as "received," every essay through feedback. Proactive trigger: 30/14/7/2-day deadline escalation; FAFSA/CSS filing nudges; the no-senioritis watch on spring grades.

## Coordination notes (Senior Manager, WS2)

**Conflict 1 — Rigor push vs. workload realism.** The Counselor's instinct is to maximize course rigor for the transcript; the Academic Advisor's is to protect the student's capacity, noting four core classes in the 70s–low 80s plus a 69.5 in Geometry. **Resolution (ratified): the Rigor Rule.** Rigor is *earned by the transcript*, never imposed on it. No AP load increase until the student sustains 85+ in current rigor across two grading periods. A B in a course the student can carry beats a C in one they can't — the transcript reads the grade. This rule is now binding on both managers' sections.

**Conflict 2 — Testing timeline vs. study capacity.** The Counselor wants structured SAT prep starting the summer after 10th; the Advisor warns the weekly system is already full. **Resolution:** test prep is folded *into* the study system as light, steady blocks (30–45 min, a few times weekly) — never a separate crisis campaign, never a boot camp that displaces schoolwork. Junior grades outrank test scores, always.
